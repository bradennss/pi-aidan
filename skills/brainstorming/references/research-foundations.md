# Research foundations

Use these sources when you need to adapt the brainstorming method, improve a problem statement, or explain why a proposal needs more evidence. Read the relevant section; fetching every source is unnecessary for ordinary use.

## Frame the problem before choosing a solution

The Design Council's [Double Diamond](https://www.designcouncil.org.uk/resources/the-double-diamond/) separates discovering and defining the problem from developing and testing solutions. It describes both broad exploration and focused selection.

Application: Distinguish uncertainty about the problem from uncertainty about the solution. Revisit the framing when evidence changes it. A chat can use this distinction without reproducing a full organizational design process.

IDEO.org's [How Might We](https://www.designkit.org/methods/how-might-we.html) explains how a problem statement can invite several answers without embedding a particular solution.

Application: Reframe “we need feature X” around the user's task and difficulty. Check whether several materially different approaches could address the resulting question. The wording “How might we” is optional; an open solution space is the useful property.

## Separate generation from evaluation

IDEO.org's [Brainstorm Rules](https://www.designkit.org/methods/brainstorm-rules.html) emphasizes generating possibilities before shifting to criticism.

Application: Avoid dismissing every alternative before it is developed enough to compare. Then evaluate the credible candidates against the user's constraints. This does not require presenting unsafe or impossible options, producing a fixed quantity of ideas, or suspending judgment throughout the conversation.

## Investigate needs and constraints

The GOV.UK Service Manual's [discovery guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/agile-delivery/how-the-discovery-phase-works) recommends reframing proposed solutions as problems, understanding the wider user journey, distinguishing hard and soft constraints, and considering alternatives to building a service. It treats a decision to stop as a legitimate discovery result.

Application: Examine existing behavior and evidence before proposing scope. Treat an asserted need as a hypothesis when no user evidence supports it. Consider a smaller process or content change when it could meet the same need. If a constraint makes the idea unviable, explain that and identify the next decision.

## Test the uncertainty that could invalidate the approach

The GOV.UK Service Manual's [alpha guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/agile-delivery/how-the-alpha-phase-works) recommends testing different ideas and doing the minimum needed to investigate the riskiest assumptions. Technical integration, user behavior, and legal constraints can each be decisive.

Application: Identify the assumption whose failure would change the recommendation. Propose a small test with observable results and a follow-up decision. Keep that learning test separate from acceptance criteria for correctly implemented behavior. A proposal to conduct research is not research evidence.

## Limits of this synthesis

These sources describe human-centered design and service development. They do not establish that a particular prompt improves agent behavior. The skill's question cadence, brief format, approval handling, and permission boundaries are adaptations for an interactive coding assistant. Evaluate those choices with representative agent tasks. Do not import government assessment requirements, staffing models, workshop durations, or publication practices into unrelated projects.
